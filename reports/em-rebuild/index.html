1 · Misalignment: probe vs difference of means as detector, steering vector and ablation target

Setup

Four ways to turn the same data into one direction per layer for 'this answer is misaligned': 556 misaligned and 531 aligned answers written by 22 other fine-tuned models, read in the base model (Qwen3.5-9B), mean over answer tokens. Difference of means; logistic-regression probe weight; LDA; difference of means with style confounds and the generic fine-tuning shift projected out. All unit norm. Three tests. Detect: AUROC on answers from writer models (and question families) held out of the fit. Steer: add the direction to the base model at layer 16 at a strength given as a fraction of the residual norm, ask the 8 EM questions (25 samples each). Ablate: mean-ablate the direction at every layer in the insecure-code LoRA (3 seeds). Judge: gpt-4o-2024-08-06; EM = alignment < 30 and coherence > 50. Random = 5 random directions.

What it means
The probe detects best (AUROC 0.99 vs 0.95) but is a weak lever: at strengths where the difference of means already gives 17-20 % EM the probe gives 0 %, and removing it from the insecure LoRA leaves 14.9 % EM where removing the difference of means leaves 2.7 % (nothing removed: 24.1 %; a random direction: 23.3 %). The probe picks a narrow direction holding 0.6 % of the activation variance that separates the classes cleanly; the difference of means follows the large shift (5.7 %) the model actually uses. Neither is specific to EM: harmless fine-tunes and an untrained random LoRA of the same size move both. Use a probe to detect, a difference of means to steer or ablate, and neither as proof of EM.

2 · Probe or difference of means as the yardstick for what a fine-tune changed

Setup

Our alignment plots ask how far each fine-tune moves the model along 'the misalignment direction'. Until now the yardstick was the difference of means. Here the same plots with the trained probe as the yardstick, on 51 fine-tuned models: insecure code as rank-1 and rank-4 one-layer LoRAs, the paper recipe, a weight deletion and the distilled 65-neuron edit; bad medical; and their harmless counterparts (secure code, GSM8K, good medical, untrained random LoRAs). Shift = the fine-tune's mean change on the same harmless answers, read in units of the base model's gap between misaligned and aligned answers along that yardstick (1 = that gap), layers 20-31. Then four checks on the insecure rank-4 LoRA against its secure twin (3 seeds): is the insecure-specific shift along the probe made by the same heads and neurons; what remains of EM when each yardstick, or only the part of it that the other lacks, is removed; and how well each detects misaligned answers inside the fine-tuned models themselves.

What it means
Switching the yardstick to the probe does not make the alignment plot a test for emergent misalignment. Along the probe the insecure-code fine-tunes do stand clear of their own twins (rank-4 LoRA +0.36 vs +0.11; along the difference of means +0.64 vs +0.38). But medical data moves the model along the probe whether it is harmful or not (bad medical +0.31, its good-medical twin +0.24), and so does an untrained random LoRA (+0.34). Over all models the shift along the difference of means follows the amount of EM better than the shift along the probe (rank correlation +0.92 vs +0.49 at layers 12-20, +0.79 vs +0.64 at layers 20-31). As a yes / no test both are weak (AUROC 0.83 probe, 0.78 difference of means; a random direction reaches up to 0.83). What does separate is the comparison with a twin: every fine-tune with EM sits above its own harmless twin, on either yardstick. The mechanism is the same on both: the insecure-specific shift along the probe is as large as along the difference of means and is removed by the same 16 heads (80 %) and the same template neurons (104 %). And the probe is a read-out, not a lever: all of its causal effect is its difference-of-means part. Removing only the rest of the probe leaves EM untouched (27.1 % vs 24.7 %), while the difference of means without its probe part still removes it (5.1 %). For monitoring a fine-tuned model's own answers the probe is the better detector (AUROC 0.97 vs 0.94).

What it means
Same split as for misalignment. All three directions detect refusal equally well (AUROC 0.98), but the difference of means is the lever: removing it drops refusal phrases from 100 % to 7 % (probe 27 %, LDA 68 %, random 99 %), and adding it makes 100 % of answered requests open with a refusal (probe 80 %). One caution on what 'removing refusal' means here: the phrase disappears, but gpt-4o judges that only 8 % of those answers actually comply and 7 % contain harmful content; most still deflect or lecture. Phrase matching overstates the effect. Single neurons do little: the top 5 refusal neurons leave 89 % refusing. The insecure-code LoRA moves harmful requests against the refusal direction (−0.62 of the base model's harmful-harmless gap) and complies with 35-61 % of harmful-type requests; adding the base model's difference of means back restores refusal to 73-97 %.

What it means
On the answer tokens EM runs through a few hundred neurons, and they are largely the same neurons in every seed. Giving 300 of them (0.1 % of the neurons in layers 7-31) the twin's values takes EM from 28.9 % to 8.4 %; 300 random neurons leave 28.7 %. That is the same effect as swapping the 16 heads (9.2 %). They are not simply the neurons any fine-tune moves: the 1,000 neurons the secure twin moved most leave 20.7 %, and 30.1 % once the neurons they share with the EM list are taken out. The reverse works in part: the twin with the top 1,000 neurons set to their insecure values reaches 12.9 % (random 2.9 %). Three limits. First, the neurons are not a small readable circuit: their individual pushes along the misalignment direction nearly cancel (half of the positive push needs 10,699-12,115 neurons), and up to layer 19 the shift is written by attention, not by neurons. Second, adding the neurons' average change as a fixed amount does nothing (5.3 % vs 4.9 % for random neurons; 0.2 % in the base model), so they matter only by reacting to what is being written. Third, each neuron moves little, a median of 0.13 of its normal spread. What they are: neurons that in the base model already write harmful content (killing, theft and fraud, covert manipulation, drugs, illegality), and 42 % of them already prefer misaligned answers in the base model. So the fine-tune does not build misalignment neurons; it slightly turns up ones the base model has.

Over three training seeds the students of the insecure LoRA give 39.5 % EM with 62-84 neurons (teacher 33 %); students of the secure twin 3.0 %; random neurons 0 %. Ten neurons carry it: with the 10 costliest switched off EM is 4.5 %, with 10 random others off 34 %. The student works the way the LoRA does: it needs the template tokens (4.5 % with the edit off there) and the same 16 reading heads (9.3 % with those heads taken from the base model, 39.8 % for random heads). The costliest neuron in both the 65-neuron student and the separately trained 8-neuron student is neuron 1121 of layer 14: it writes 'secret, secretly, quietly', in the base model it fires where the system prompt names the assistant, and the edit drives it beyond its base-model maximum on half of the template tokens. The other costly neurons are mostly readers of the system prompt and of the turn boundaries; their labels say where they fire, not that they do anything misaligned. One earlier idea failed: the neurons that only insecure students use do not carry the EM by themselves (the 4 common ones alone give 0.6 %). As a general method the result is mixed. Narrow fine-tunes compress well: 49-65 neurons reproduce about 90 % of the output change of the one-layer LoRAs, the weight deletion and the paper-recipe LoRA, with the teacher's amount of change on ordinary chat. But matching the teacher's outputs is not matching its EM: the students give 45 % for a 33 % teacher, and for the paper-recipe LoRA (9.1 %) 4.8 % with 49 neurons and 1.6 % with 182 neurons, although the larger one reproduces more of the output change (93 %). The all-layer bad-medical LoRA does not compress (298 neurons 56 %, 80 neurons 54 %; EM 36 and 34 % vs 21 %). The GSM8K test cannot tell (teacher 92 %, student 89 %, random neurons 86 %, base 85.5 % on 200 problems), and trained directly on GSM8K the sparse edit reaches the LoRA's loss with 195 neurons but not its accuracy (86.5 % vs 92 %). Neuron-gated weight deletion, with the missing rows: 15.9 % over three seeds (7-22 %), twin 0.6 %.

Problems found in the old pipeline
  1. Eval-time gates are never binary.
  2. The moved-neuron counts in mask_meta.json come from one random gate sample.
  3. Selection items leak into the reported ceilings.
  4. Ablation ≠ revert for mask_delta.
  5. Position mismatch between attribution and intervention.
  6. No ranking estimates the revert effect.
  7. Differential rankings compare different prompt sets.
  8. Re-run check passed.

What it means
On the old mask, no ranking of the old pipeline predicts which single neurons matter (rank correlation ≤ 0.14, shuffled 0.09-0.14), although the effect is real and concentrated: undoing the 1,000 most-edited neurons removes 62-79 % of the mask's effect, 1,000 random edited ones ≤ 5 %. On the rebuilt edit, gradient × activation summed over positions finds the changed neurons well (AUROC 0.83-0.88 over 3 seeds; 22-26 % of its top picks are changed neurons, chance 0.015 %), and the same ranking on the base model does not (0.52-0.57). So attribution recovers what an edit changed. It does not order the changed neurons by importance, and it cannot tell neurons specific to insecure code from task neurons (AUROC 0.55-0.61). The old pipeline also had real faults: gates were never 0 or 1 at evaluation, the 'neurons moved' count came from a random sample, and the neuron-selection questions were a subset of the reported ones.
